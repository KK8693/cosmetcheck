## Understanding ANVISA (Brazil)

Brazil's cosmetics regulator, **ANVISA** (Agência Nacional de Vigilância Sanitária), operates under **RDC 907/2024** — the most recent comprehensive regulation governing cosmetic products.

### What's Banned

ANVISA maintains a strict list of prohibited substances in cosmetics. Some of the most commonly encountered bans include:

- **Hydroquinone** — Banned in concentrations above 2% since 2001; completely prohibited in skin-lightening products
- **Mercury and mercury compounds** — Prohibited due to neurotoxicity
- **Formaldehyde** — Classified as a carcinogen; banned as a direct ingredient
- **Corticosteroids** — Including clobetasol, betamethasone, and hydrocortisone in cosmetic formulations
- **Retinoic acid (tretinoin)** — Prescription-only; prohibited in OTC cosmetics

### What's Restricted

Some ingredients are allowed only within specific concentration limits:

- **Salicylic acid** — Limited to 2% in leave-on products, 3% in rinse-off
- **Benzoyl peroxide** — Maximum 10% concentration
- **Alpha-arbutin** — Skin-lightening agent with concentration caps
- **Titanium dioxide nanoparticles** — Specific labeling and concentration requirements

### Registration Requirements

Most cosmetics in Brazil fall under **"Grade 2"** (low risk) and don't require pre-market approval. However, you must:

1. Have a **local legal representative** in Brazil
2. Submit product notifications through ANVISA's online system
3. Ensure Portuguese labeling meets NRV requirements
4. Maintain a **Product Information File (PIF)** available for inspection

## Understanding COFEPRIS (Mexico)

Mexico's regulatory body, **COFEPRIS** (Comisión Federal para la Protección contra Riesgos Sanitarios), governs cosmetics under **NOM-141-SSA1** and related standards.

### What's Banned

COFEPRIS shares many bans with ANVISA but has some unique restrictions:

- **Lead acetate** — Prohibited in hair dyes
- **Hexachlorophene** — Banned following historical infant poisoning incidents
- **Chloramphenicol** — Antibiotic with risk of aplastic anemia
- **Phenacetin** — Associated with kidney disease and cancer

### What's Restricted

Mexican restrictions often mirror Brazilian ones but with slightly different limits:

- **p-Phenylenediamine (PPD)** — Hair dye ingredient with strict concentration limits
- **Resorcinol** — Limited in hair coloring products
- **Ammonia** — Concentration caps in hair products

### Labeling Requirements

Mexican cosmetics must include:

- **Spanish labeling** (mandatory)
- **INCI ingredient list** in descending order of concentration
- **Expiration date** or PAO symbol
- **Batch number** and manufacturer information
- **Warnings** for restricted ingredients

## Common Compliance Mistakes (And How to Avoid Them)

### Mistake 1: Assuming EU/US Compliance Equals LATAM Compliance

A product legal in the EU or US is **not automatically legal** in Brazil or Mexico. Hydroquinone is banned in Brazil but allowed in the EU at low concentrations. Always check specifically for your target market.

### Mistake 2: Using Supplier Ingredient Lists Without Verification

Many manufacturers provide incomplete or outdated ingredient information. A "fragrance" or "preservative" blanket term can hide banned substances. Always demand full INCI disclosure.

### Mistake 3: Ignoring Labeling Requirements

Even if your ingredients are compliant, incorrect labeling can block your shipment. Portuguese labels for Brazil, Spanish labels for Mexico — both mandatory.

### Mistake 4: Not Monitoring Regulatory Changes

Brazil updates its cosmetics regulations approximately every 2-3 years. Mexico follows a similar cadence. A compliant product today may not be compliant next year.

For a small cosmetics brand shipping $10,000 worth of inventory, a single compliance failure can erase your entire LATAM profit margin.
